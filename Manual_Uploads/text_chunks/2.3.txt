The academic literature on automated irrigation has been growing consistently since the early 2000s, when soil moisture sensors became cheap enough to consider in student and research projects. The most relevant threads for this thesis are soil moisture based control, multi-parameter sensing, cloud connectivity in research prototypes, and closed-loop fertigation.

Soil moisture control is the most studied approach. The foundational idea — measure volumetric water content in the soil, trigger irrigation when it drops below a threshold, stop when it reaches the target — has been validated across a large number of field studies in different crops and climates [3]. Results consistently show water savings compared to timer-based irrigation, typically in the 20 to 50 percent range, with equal or better crop yields when thresholds are set correctly for the specific crop and soil.

The practical limitation has always been calibration and long-term stability. Capacitive soil moisture sensors are sensitive to soil texture, organic matter content, and especially to the presence of fertilizer salts in the soil water. A sensor calibrated in clean sandy soil reads differently in that same soil after several fertilizer applications. Periodic recalibration is required for accurate results. Some research systems address this by placing multiple sensors at different depths and averaging, but this increases both cost and the number of things that can fail.

Multi-parameter sensing has been explored as a more complete picture of plant water and nutrient status. Systems that combine soil moisture, temperature, electrical conductivity, and sometimes pH try to make better decisions than any single measurement allows. This is directionally similar to what Smart Flow 2.0 does, though at the water source rather than in the soil. Monitoring TDS and pH at the point of irrigation tells you about the quality of the water going in, not the current state of the soil. These are complementary measurements — both matter for healthy plant growth, and neither alone tells the full story.

Cloud connectivity became standard in research prototypes around 2017 to 2018. Before that, most published student systems stored data locally or used basic SMS connections for alerts. After ESP8266 and ESP32 became widely available, Firebase, ThingSpeak, and Blynk emerged as the most common platforms. Firebase appears frequently in more recent work [14] because the free tier is generous, real-time synchronization is well-suited to dashboards that show live sensor data, and Android libraries make mobile app development straightforward without needing a backend server. Smart Flow 2.0 uses Firebase Realtime Database for exactly these reasons.

Fertigation — delivering fertilizer dissolved in the irrigation water — is where academic research prototypes lag most behind commercial systems. The general approach in most research projects that mention fertigation is to activate a fertilizer injection pump on a fixed timer during irrigation cycles. This is simple to implement but does not adapt to actual fertilizer concentration in the water. If the fertilizer tank is more concentrated than usual, or if the flow rate is lower than expected, the crop receives more or less fertilizer than intended and the system does not know.

Closed-loop fertigation, where the system measures TDS continuously and adjusts fertilizer injection to maintain a target concentration, is rarely seen in research prototype papers at this price level. Commercial systems like Netafim have had this capability for a long time, but in the low-cost microcontroller prototype space, combining real-time TDS measurement with active valve control for fertilizer adjustment is not well represented in published work. This is one area where Smart Flow 2.0 contributes something genuinely different from most existing prototypes — not just a cheaper version of the same approach, but a different control architecture.

The broader category of IoT irrigation systems built around ESP32 or ESP8266 with Firebase backends is well represented in recent publications from 2020 to 2023. These systems typically include mobile app control, soil moisture sensing, and basic scheduling. They work and they validate the concept of low-cost connected irrigation. But they do not include water quality sensing or fertilizer control. They represent the typical boundary of what student-level prototypes attempt. Smart Flow 2.0 pushed past that boundary on two fronts: using more capable microcontrollers for the sensing layer, and adding a closed-loop control subsystem that goes beyond simple relay actuation.
